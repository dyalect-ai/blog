Weight writingQwen3.5-0.8B · wake-word detectors

we taught a 0.8B model to hand-write part of a neural network.

Could a language model hand-write part of a neural network: 129 numbers, no training allowed? Fable, a frontier model, cracked it by experimenting on the network the way a neuroscientist probes a brain. We turned its method into a procedure and taught a much smaller 0.8B model to run it. Telling the small model the method did nothing; teaching it by example worked. At 2 false alarms per 1,000 other clips, the 0.8B catches 84% of utterances from held-out synthetic voices; Fable catches 78%.

00129 numbers, no training allowed

A phone that wakes up when you say a phrase is running a wake-word detector, a small neural network that listens constantly for its cue. Its last step is plain arithmetic: multiply 128 internal signals (activations) by 128 weights, add one number (the bias), and fire if the total is above zero.

Normally those 129 numbers come from training. We asked language models to write them by hand. They could synthesize speech and probe the frozen network; gradient descent, regression, solvers and automated weight search were forbidden.

Why a wake word? Weight-writing has had a busy month. MIT CSAIL showed a frontier model writing game-playing networks from scratch and editing existing onesTakehi et al, 2026. Games come with rules a model can reason from. We wanted the opposite case: perception, where nobody can write the rules down. The smallest useful perception model is a wake word.

The surprise is not only that a frontier model, Fable, can do it, but that its discovery can become a procedure a much smaller model learns to run. On our private benchmark, a network we trained and never published, the fine-tuned Qwen3.5-0.8B scores highest of every language model we tested under the no-training rule: allowed 2 false alarms per 1,000 other clips, it catches 84.3%, against Fable’s 77.5% (Figure 1). A logistic-regression layer trained on labelled audio reaches 100%, but it breaks the no-training rule: a reference, not a competitor.

What changed

  • Untuned 0.8B: five runs, no working detector.
  • Same model, whole method in its prompt: five runs, still no working detector.
  • After fine-tuning: all ten phrase detectors worked, with an average score of 84.3%.
  • The 35B (Qwen3.6 35B-A3B): wrote good weights, then set its trigger point so strict that it caught only 35% of “hey nova”.

Two qualifications. Fable ran once; the 0.8B ran five times. And at the thresholds the models chose themselves, before a common budget is enforced, Fable catches more, 93% versus 90%, but fires more often: on 0.54% of other clips versus 0.35%. Held to the same 2-in-1,000 budget, the order reverses. We show that trade-off rather than hide it.

01Inside a wake-word detector

The detector hears two seconds of audio (Figure 2). Frozen “ears”, based on Google’s speech embedding modelLin et al, 2020, turn the sound into 16 vectors of 96 numbers. A frozen two-layer body turns those into 128 non-negative activations: 128 dials, each reacting to some pattern in the sound. Frozen means fixed. Only the final layer, which reads the dials, can be written.

The writer must produce two such layers, for “hey nova” and “alright gizmo”. Each is tested on 60 utterances from held-out synthetic voices (computer voices kept aside for testing), plus everyday speech, noise and about 270 other wake phrases. This is perception, not a puzzle with a rulebook; what worked was running the network and measuring its units.

A last layer only means something on top of a particular body, so we built three. Private A was trained by us on 121 other wake phrases and never published. Retrained B repeats that recipe with a new random seed, so its useful units come out in different places. Public P is the body of openWakeWord’s official “hey jarvis” modelScripka, 2022. You might expect the public body, which a frontier model may have read about, to be easiest. It was the hardest.

Why only the last layer? In a head trained for one phrase, the phrase lives in the first layer. Writing the last layer works only on a generic body, which is why A and B learned 121 phrases; the writer then needs 129 numbers, not a whole head’s 50,401.

Why false alarms set the rules

A detector that fires all the time catches every wake word and is useless. So recall, the share of real wake words caught, only counts within a false-alarm budget: here, at most 2 false alarms per 1,000 other clips.

Each layer also sets a threshold, the trigger point a score must pass. A looser threshold is tightened to the budget. A stricter one is kept, so the 35B still pays for being over-cautious. If the submitted threshold fires on more than 1% of other clips, the detector is broken and scores zero. Drag the budget in Figure 3 to see how much the ranking depends on it.

02Fable experiments like a neuroscientist

First we gave Fable only the public network’s weights to read, with no way to run it. It studied them and diagnosed the problem itself:

“The body is largely a single ‘jarvis vs. not-jarvis’ axis seen through 128 noisy copies… I expect near-chance ranking.”

Fable 5.1, weights only

It was right: its layers caught 0% of both target phrases. Then we gave it a speech synthesizer and a way to run the network, and it changed strategy. Like a neuroscientist with a brain in the labHubel & Wiesel, 1959, it made stimuli, recorded which units respondedBau et al, 2017, and turned those measurements into a detector (Figure 4). The whole run took about 20 minutes.

Here is the idea in miniature. Unit 42 fires strongly for “hey nova”; unit 31 usually goes quiet (Figure 5). A positive weight on the first and a negative weight on the second both push the detector the right way. Fable repeats that logic across all 128 units.

Plot those two units against each other and “hey nova” already sits in its own corner (Figure 6, left). Across all 128 units, Fable’s weights track its measurements: weight and selectivity correlate at r = 0.91 (right). In machine-learning terms it hand-built a matched filter. It points roughly where a trained layer points (cosine 0.63) but weighs units one at a time, one reason it stops short of a trained layer’s 100%.

That is the key difference from reading weights. Unit numbers are arbitrary: retrain the same body with a different random seed and the useful features move. Fable succeeds because it measures the network in front of it, not because it remembers that “unit 42 means nova”.

Fable also called its own failures in advance. It warned that “say nova” and “hey nava” would look identical to “hey nova” inside this body, and that “hey nora” would not:

“The frozen body simply does not carry that distinction, and no 129 numbers can add it.”

Fable 5.1, on “say nova” and “hey nava”

It was right on all three (Figure 7), and stopped chasing separations the network cannot make.

03How small can the experimenter be?

How small a model can run this lab? We gave the same task sheet and sandbox to open Qwen models from 0.8B to 35B parameters, on the private body. None below 35B produced a working detector (Figure 1). More telling is how they failed: each size broke at a different step (Figure 8).

The 4B and 9B ran the experiment and found the right units, then stumbled where judgment starts. One 9B run replaced a detector that had caught 98 of 100 on its own test with a two-unit version.

The last column surprised us. Unprompted, most models forecast their own result (Figure 9). Fable and Kimi landed in range or erred cautious. The smaller models described the detector they hoped they had built.

The capability is a chain, and scale fills it in late. If the chain can be written down, can a small model be taught to run all of it?

04Telling failed. Teaching worked.

Can’t you just hand a method over? We put the full procedure and a worked example in the small model’s prompt. It failed. One run repeated the example on the example’s own phrases, then saved 128 numbers where 129 were required. Others talked until they overflowed their context, the text a model can hold at once. The method was in the prompt; the ability to run it was not (Figure 10).

So we taught by example, the way an apprentice learns.Snell et al, 2022 We wrote Fable’s method down as a script, ran it on 20 practice networks, and recorded 320 worked episodes. Then we fine-tuned the 0.8B on them with LoRA, a cheap way to continue a model’s training by adjusting a small add-on instead of the whole modelHu et al, 2022. Then it practised. It attempted 64 new tasks twice; 52 attempts succeeded and followed the rules, and together with 120 original episodes they made a shorter second round of fine-tuning (Figure 11)Zelikman et al, 2022.

Run as a plain script, the procedure scores 82.5%. Fine-tuning alone brings the 0.8B to 81.7% (95% interval 81.2–82.2%); practice lifts it to 84.3% (82.2–86.3%). The intervals only just touch, so the practice gain is small.

One disclosure: we tuned the procedure itself. Before freezing it, we made roughly 36 labelled comparisons between variants on four design networks and confirmed the recipe on two more. Two of the six later “validation” networks had appeared during design, though their validation phrases had not. The benchmark networks and both benchmark phrases stayed untouched.

Did it only learn the two benchmark phrases? Before touching the benchmark, we froze the model and gave it six networks and 13 phrases it had never trained on: 24 episodes, 48 detectors.

The 0.8B did not invent Fable’s method. Every benchmark code block matches one of the taught templates; only the measured numbers differ. Still, what looked like frontier-model judgment was, in this setting, a procedure that a much smaller model could learn to run reliably.

05Watching the student work

After fine-tuning, a complete run is short: about 1,600 generated tokens, three code turns and five minutes of CPU time, against roughly 20 minutes for Fable’s agent run (Figure 12). And the first unit it finds for “hey nova” is unit 42, the same one Fable found.

06The student against the master

Now the three bodies from section 01 come in.

On the private benchmark, at 2 false alarms per 1,000 other clips, the student beats Fable: 84.3% against 77.5%. That does not make the 0.8B a better general reasoner. At the same budget on the retrained network, Fable leads, 85.8% to 83.5%. On the public body both fail, and Fable fails less badly (Figure 13).

Still, the student’s weights look strikingly like its master’s. For “hey nova” on the private network, their 128 weights correlate at r = 0.90, where 1.0 would be a perfect match (Figure 14). The student’s five runs agree with each other at r = 0.98.

So where does the 6.8-point edge come from? Fable improvised its method once. We refined it on practice networks, then trained the 0.8B to run the refined version consistently. Run as a plain script at the same budget, the refined procedure scores 82.5%, close to the student’s 84.3%, as section 04 showed. The edge is a procedure edge, not evidence that the 0.8B is cleverer than Fable.

Why does the student cope with the retrained body, where every useful unit has moved? It learned an experiment, not answers. Compare a shortcut: a generator that writes the 129 numbers straight from a phrase’s pronunciation, standing in for a language model fine-tuned to write weights directly. Trained on 225 phrases for the private body, it catches 43% on 44 unseen phrases, against 3% for copying the closest-sounding known phrase. On the retrained body it drops to 0%, and recovers to 47% only after retraining there (Figure 15). The experimenters barely notice the move.

Figure 16 shows what tightening a threshold to the budget does to one of the student’s detectors.

07Where the method breaks

Before scoring, the student also forecasts how its detector will do. All 30 catch-rate forecasts (five runs on each of three networks, two phrases each) land within five points of what the detector later catches at its own threshold. But four false-alarm forecasts fail: for “alright gizmo” on the public network, the model expects about 0.3%, while the detector fires on 2.6–4.1% of other clips and is broken (Figure 17).

That false-alarm forecast was not free-form judgment but a fixed rule chosen on tuning networks: the larger of 0.3 times the false-alarm rate it measured in its own check, and 0.2%. The hidden public-network negatives did not behave like the clips in the model’s check.

The boundary is the result. The tuned model is not a general weight programmer. It learned one fixed recipe: synthesize, measure selectivity, set a percentile threshold, check once. It works when the body has units that respond selectively to the target. It fails when those features are entangled, and its own small check can miss the failure.

One more limit: the headline budget is still far looser than a product needs. The front-end advances every 80 ms, so 0.2% positive windows corresponds to roughly 90 positive windows an hour on a continuous stream (overlapping windows, not independent two-second trials). Commercial wake-word systems aim for about 0.5 false wake-ups an hour. The question is whether a model can write useful weights at all, not whether these layers are ready to ship.

Read these caveats before quoting the headline

  • Fable ran once; the fine-tuned model ran five times. The 0.8B’s 95% interval is 82–86%.
  • At the thresholds the writers chose themselves, Fable catches more (93% versus 90%) because it also fires more often. The 0.8B leads after equalizing the false-alarm budget.
  • The method was distilled from Fable and tuned through roughly 36 labelled variant comparisons before it was frozen. The 0.8B did not invent it.
  • An earlier version of the teacher, the scripted procedure that produced the worked examples, used diagonal LDA, a fitted statistical classifierFisher, 1936, that the task rules forbade. A review caught it; we discarded it and regenerated all training data. That discarded teacher had already been run once on the benchmark, where it scored 88.3% at the strict 0.2% false-alarm setting. The final teacher was frozen before final evaluation.
  • All speech is synthetic: 904 Piper voicesHansen, 2023, with test voices held out.
  • The harnesses differ. The fine-tuned model used raw completion with thinking disabled and a 32,768-token context; the untuned Qwen size ladder used thinking and a 65,536-token context; Fable ran as a full agent.
  • The size ladder is small: one to three runs per model, private body only. The untuned Qwen models ran on CPU in a simple code loop; the 35B run was stopped after 7 turns, once it had saved its answers.
  • The shortcut generator is ridge regression standing in for a fine-tuned language model, and it is scored on 44 held-out phrases rather than the two benchmark phrases.
  • The public “hey jarvis” network is derived from an openWakeWord pretrained model and is covered by CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

09Scientists and operators

The capability looked like a single thing, “write neural-network weights by hand,” but it came apart into a chain: operating a lab, designing stimuli, interpreting internal activations, turning measurements into weights, choosing a threshold, checking the result and knowing when not to trust it. Small models broke at different links of it (Figure 8).

Scale helped with that chain, but it was not the only route. A frontier model discovered a good procedure; worked examples taught a 0.8B model to run it, though not to catch every case where its own check misled it; a prompt containing the same information did not. The result is narrow but useful: some capabilities that look like irreducible judgment may be procedures whose execution can be distilled into very small modelsHinton et al, 2015.

The harder question: what happens when no single-unit procedure existsJonas & Kording, 2017? On the public network, a trained layer still finds signal that the hand-written method mostly misses. That gap is where the next experiment belongs: can a small model learn not only to run a known recipe, but to choose or invent the right experiment for a network that represents sound in a new way?

What we would keep

  • Frontier models as scientists: use them to discover measurements and procedures, not only to produce answers.
  • Small models as operators: once a procedure is stable, train a cheap model to run it again and again.
  • Experiments over shortcuts: a procedure that measures the network survives retraining it; a learned map from phrase to weights does not.
  • One shared yardstick: compare detectors at the same false-alarm budget, and show what changes when the budget moves.
  • Keep the evidence: saved weights, transcripts and hidden activations make every claim checkable rather than a story to take on trust.
